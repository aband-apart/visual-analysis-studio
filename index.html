Before you begin

There is no single “correct” response.

Strong analysis connects an idea to visible evidence. Specific observations are more useful than art-history vocabulary.

Optional helpArt words explained Open +
Perspective
A way to make flat images appear to have depth.
Horizon line
The viewer’s eye level within an image.
Vanishing point
The place where receding parallel lines seem to meet.
Implied texture
A surface painted or drawn to look touchable.
Unity
The sense that the parts belong together.
Variety
Differences that create interest or contrast.
Scale
The size of something compared with a person or familiar object.
Proportion
How the sizes of parts relate to one another.
Balance
How visual weight is distributed across the work.
Symmetrical
Balanced with similar parts on both sides.
Asymmetrical
Balanced with different parts on each side.
Emphasis
The part that attracts your attention first.
Movement
The path your eyes follow through the work.
Rhythm
Repetition that creates a visual beat or pattern.
01

Begin with evidence

Step 1: Description — “What do you see?”

Naming what's physically there.

Guiding questions +
  • Is the work two-dimensional (flat) or three-dimensional (has depth)?
  • What is the medium, and how big is the work?
  • Line: soft or hard, jagged or straight, expressive or mechanical?
  • Shape: big or small, hard-edged or soft? What's in front vs. background?
  • Mass & volume: illusion of weight/depth (if 2D), or real space occupied (if 3D)?
  • Space: perspective used? Horizon line? Vanishing point?
  • Texture: actual (touchable) or implied (painted to look that way)?
  • Color: what colors/scheme? Overall light, medium, or dark?

Try naming three things you can point to in the image.

02

Find relationships

Step 2: Analysis — “How is it arranged?”

Explaining how the parts relate.

Guiding questions +
  • Unity & variety: what ties it together vs. adds interest?
  • Scale & proportion: bigger/smaller than real life? Parts sized correctly to each other?
  • Balance: symmetrical or asymmetrical?
  • Emphasis: where does your eye go first, and why?
  • Movement: what guides your eye through the work?
  • Rhythm: are shapes, colors, or lines repeated?

Explain how one visual choice affects where you look.

03

Make a claim

Step 3: Interpretation — “What might it say?”

Proposing what it might mean.

Guiding questions +
  • What feeling or idea does the work communicate?
  • What might it mean to the artist vs. to you today?
  • Could it support more than one valid interpretation?
  • What evidence from Steps 1–2 backs up your reading?

Use “might” or “could,” then support your idea with evidence.

04

Take a position

Step 4: Evaluation — “Is it effective? What do I think?”

Judging its effect and value.

Guiding questions +
  • Does it succeed at what it seems to be trying to do?
  • What do you feel when you look at it?
  • Why do you feel that way — tie it to something specific from Steps 1–3?

State your judgment, then explain what led you there.

Finish your session

Take your writing with you.
